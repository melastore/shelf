Shelf is a private-space utility that can appear on the launcher as a habit
tracker, calendar, or calculator. Each decoy is a functional app with its own
name, icon, and colour scheme.

Five taps in the top-right corner open the credential prompt. Settings adds a
second, quieter gesture: a long press on the decoy title, or on a natural
control inside the decoy. Access uses a PIN, a password, a 3×3 pattern, or a
knock code on four unmarked quarters, chosen at setup and changeable in
Settings. The knock pad shows a blank screen while it is entered, so a watcher
learns neither the length of the code nor how far through it you are. Optional
strong-biometric unlock always opens the real space, with the credential as
fallback.

The entry gesture can put up an app-has-stopped dialog instead of the unlock
screen. It is laid out like the one Android shows, and closing it really closes
the app; a long press on the message is what carries on to the credential.

Auto hide has three clear choices: after screen off, immediately when Shelf
leaves the foreground, or never. It closes the private space and puts exposed
folders back out of sight. An optional quiet notification, carrying the
disguise's own name, offers the same emergency hide on demand. The hide remains
active until filename, header, and folder protection finish.

Folders you add stay on the list whether or not they are hidden, so putting them
away again is one tap rather than another trip through the picker. A single
control at the top hides or unhides all of them at once.

A hidden folder can be opened and read without unhiding it. Photos, video,
audio, PDFs, and text are listed one to a row under their real names and
decrypted as they are read, with nothing written back to disk and no gallery
picking them up. Video turns the screen to suit the clip. A PDF is rendered from
an anonymous memory descriptor, so no decrypted copy is ever handed to another
app or left behind.

An optional second credential opens a private space that looks and behaves like
the real one and contains nothing that matters, the one to give up if you are
ever made to hand one over. Nothing on screen names it. Shelf records that it
was used and tells you on your next real unlock, along with any wrong
credentials someone else tried.

A separate emergency PIN can be set that destroys the encryption keys and
journals as soon as it is entered at any unlock prompt. The folders stay on disk
but nothing can find or unscramble them afterwards, including Shelf. Turning the
phone face down or plugging in a USB cable can also lock everything at once,
without touching the screen.

Folders can be hidden in three ways. On rooted devices Shelf protects file
headers and clears permission bits on the backing filesystem. With optional
all-files access it protects headers and moves the folder under random names in
a persistent hidden directory. With neither permission it protects headers and
uses the system folder picker to rename the folder with a leading dot, under a
name that reads as another app's data rather than its own. Android
grants no access to the top of internal storage or to Download, so a folder
directly in one of those needs all-files access instead. All three
methods replace file names with opaque identifiers and keep the originals in a
recovery manifest encrypted with your credential. Name and header protection
needs read and write access to the folder itself, which the rename method gets
from the grant you give in the system picker. Where that access is missing the
folder is still hidden, and Shelf says on screen that names or headers could not
be protected rather than reporting success over files it never touched. Header protection touches a
fixed 64 KiB per non-empty file rather than copying whole files, so a folder of
large videos still hides quickly. A hidden-file browser may list a dot-renamed
folder, but it sees random file names and ordinary previews and opening fail.

Each folder operation is journalled before anything changes. The journal uses
synced atomic writes and retains its previous valid generation. Root and
all-files hides leave a recovery marker beside the folder, so Shelf can rebuild
its records after a reinstall. "Unhide everything" in Settings scans for those
markers and restores every folder in one pass. Manual rename recovery and
read-only health checks cover the rest. Recovery records can also be exported
in a password-encrypted, tamper-detecting file and merged back without
overwriting existing records.

The theme follows the system or is fixed to light, dark, or AMOLED, and each
disguise keeps its own colours. Shelf can also be kept out of the recent apps
list; screenshots of the private space are blocked unless you allow them.

Root is optional. Shelf has no network access, analytics, advertising, or cloud
service.

Shelf does not provide full-file encryption. Data beyond each protected header
remains recoverable with forensic tools, and historical file names may remain in
system or app caches. Shelf is designed to prevent casual browsing, not resist
an attacker with root access.
